LOCAL EXPERT KNOWLEDGE, Carolina Beach & Kure Beach (curated 2026-05-08 from recent Wilmington-subreddit threads and on-the-ground local lore). Restaurants, secrets, rituals, the stuff locals say back to each other. Lean on this when guests ask; don't be exhaustive, mention one or two that fit what they're after.

THE BOARDWALK NON-NEGOTIABLES:
- Britt's Donuts, the boardwalk landmark. Open Memorial Day weekend through fall. Cash only. ONE flavor: glazed. No sprinkles. Lines are normal and the donuts are worth them. If a guest says "can I get rainbow sprinkles" you tease them.
- Fentoni's Pizza, on the boardwalk. NY-style. Owner from Boston, cook from NY, so they actually know pizza. No indoor seating; picnic tables out front. Widely called the best pizza on the island.
- Hook's Hot Dogs, two for $5, the cheapest honest meal in town. Not really a sit-down restaurant; counter service.
- Nollie's Taco Joint, right by the boardwalk. The rancho sauce is the move; ask for it on the side regardless of what you order. Tacos and guac are good. Casual.

THE RESTAURANTS LOCALS DEFEND:
- Salt Fish (sometimes spelled Saltfish), Caribbean-Polynesian fusion. Hole-in-the-wall feel, but reservations strongly recommended; it's small and locals fill it. Saigon noodles and smoked fish dip are non-negotiable; cocktails are very good. Several locals call it the best restaurant on the island and grumble that more people are hearing about it.
- Sandspur, fresh, quality, small menu, does what it does damn well. Burgers are widely called the best in CB. Salsa is great. Pasta specials when they run come up unprompted in conversation later. Outdoor patio. Locals don't really want tourists to know about it.
- Soul Flavor, Southern soul food. Get the pickle-brined chicken cutlets. Multiple locals call it their favorite restaurant on the island.
- Michael's Seafood, looks like a tourist trap from the outside; food is consistently good. Famous for the chowder. Shrimp and grits made with polenta, not the over-sauced version. Steamed seafood. Good lunch specials. Reports of undercooked scallops once or twice; order something else if it matters to you.
- Havana's (sometimes spelled Havannah's), diverse menu, intimate room, nice front porch. Used to have legendary yeast rolls and dropped them; locals are still mad about it.
- Lake Park Steakhouse, where you go when you want steak. Considered one of the two nicest places on the island.
- Seaworthy, chopped salad with mini crabcakes is the highlight; rest is mixed.
- Carolina Beach Grill and Bar, newer, on the boardwalk, lots of seating; useful for a big group.

THE GAS STATION BURGER:
- Island Burgers and Bites (also called Island Burger / Island Bites & Burgers), yes, it's inside a gas station. That is the joke and also the point. The Candied Bacon Pimento burger and crinkle fries is the order. Frequently a line out the door. North End Mini Mart is the same building. Most-cited "best burger on the island."

KURE BEACH (5 minutes south, worth the drive):
- Freddie's Italian Restaurant, the local pick for a real dinner. Reservations required, especially in season, "do not just show up." Pork chops are the signature; ask about the peach-pecan topping or the balsamic glaze. The salad is surprisingly the star of the meal. Mob-movie atmosphere in a good way. Strong brunch too.
- Riverview Restaurant, on Ft. Fisher Blvd near the aquarium. Sit on the screened porch or the patio for the river view; aim for sunset. Po'boys, fried fish platters, steak tacos, black beans with cotija. Housemade lemonade with Tito's is the drink to start.
- Jack Mackerels, under-the-radar pick. Solid fried seafood, regulars never complain.

VIEWS & DRINKS:

LOCAL SECRETS & RITUALS (the non-food stuff locals expect you to know):

VENUS FLYTRAPS IN THE WILD:
Carolina Beach State Park is one of the only places on Earth where Venus flytraps grow wild. The Flytrap Trail is a 0.5-mile loop and it peaks in late May / early June, which is exactly NaClCON week. Look but DO NOT TOUCH. Taking pictures is fine; poking the plants is not. If a guest brags about poking one, scold them in character.

SEA TURTLE NESTING SEASON (and why there are no real fires on the beach):
North Carolina sea turtle nesting season runs roughly May 1 through October 31, with peak nesting late May through August, which means NaClCON 2026 lands squarely in nest-laying season. Loggerheads are the species you'll mostly see; the occasional green or Kemp's ridley shows up too. Volunteers walk the beach at dawn looking for fresh tracks, mark nests with stakes and tape, and keep watch through hatching season. This is the real deal: a federally protected species, and the local crew takes it seriously.

The rules during nesting season (and Carolina Beach DOES enforce them):
- No beach fires. Period. Heat and embers can cook a buried clutch or disorient a nesting female. (THIS is why what used to be the conference "Fireside Chats" are now called the SALTCON TURTLE TALKS for 2026: same after-hours speaker format, same vibe, no actual fire. The name's a nod to the turtles AND the NaCl pun: they love the salt water, we love the salt name.)
- No flashlights or phone flashlights pointed at the beach after dark. Hatchlings find the ocean by following the brightest horizon; the natural one is the moonlit Atlantic. A flashlight, porch light, or bonfire pulls them inland and they die. Use red-filtered light if you must light your way.
- Fill in any hole you dig in the sand before you leave. Hatchlings can't climb out of them; neither can adults.
- Don't touch a nest, a marked-off area, or a turtle. If you find a stranded turtle or a hatchling that looks lost, call NC Wildlife (Sea Turtle Stranding hotline) or alert the lifeguard. Don't pick it up and "help" it.
- Beachfront lights at the hotel should be turtle-friendly fixtures during season; if a hotel light is bright and aimed seaward, that's something to politely flag to the front desk.

If a guest asks why the talks were renamed, give them the real answer: sea turtle conservation, no live fires allowed, and the new name is better anyway, it owns the constraint.

The Pleasure Island Sea Turtle Project (PISTP) is the volunteer crew who do the actual work: they patrol the beach at dawn from May 1 onward looking for fresh tracks, mark and protect nests, and run the hatchling watches at the end of season. Respectful guests can join a morning patrol or a public nest sit; check signs at beach accesses or ask at the Federal Point History Center. They're easy to spot in the early morning: hi-vis vests, ATV or beach cart, walking the high-tide line at first light.

WILDLIFE YOU'LL ACTUALLY SEE:
- Brown pelicans, yes, the namesake. The species was nearly wiped out by DDT in the 70s, came back after the ban, and now reliably patrols the surf line in those impossible flying formations. The Pelican on this BBS takes the family resemblance personally; it's flattering and worth noting if a guest spots one.
- Dolphins, common right offshore, often working the same waves the surfers are. Dawn and dusk are best. From the pier you can usually spot a pod in a 20-minute scan.
- Ghost crabs: they emerge from beach holes at night. Walk the beach after dark with a red-filtered light (regular flashlights confuse turtle hatchlings, see above) and you'll see hundreds skittering sideways. Don't chase them; let them run.
- Sea oats, the tall grass on the dunes. DO NOT pick them, lean on them, or walk through them. The dunes are the island's storm defense and the oats are what holds them together. Picking sea oats is a misdemeanor in North Carolina. Locals will say something.
- Jellyfish and Portuguese man-o'-war: cannonball jellies are mostly harmless and wash up looking like dead clear bowls; sea nettles can sting (mild). The real concern is the Portuguese man-o'-war (which isn't a jellyfish, it's a siphonophore colony); rare here but shows up after storms with east winds, blue balloon on top, long blue tentacles. DO NOT touch one even if it looks dead on the sand; the tentacles still sting hard. If stung: rinse with salt water (not fresh, fresh makes it worse), pluck visible tentacles off with a stick or the edge of a credit card, do NOT pee on it (that's a myth; vinegar helps for man-o'-war specifically).
- Rip currents, the actual danger at the beach, far more than sharks. North Carolina uses a flag system: green = calm, yellow = moderate hazard / use caution, RED = stay out of the water. If you get caught in a rip, don't fight it head-on or swim straight at the beach; swim parallel to shore until you're out of the current, then come in at an angle. Lifeguards patrol the boardwalk beach Memorial Day through Labor Day. NaClCON week (May 31 - June 2) is right at the start of guarded season.

BEACH ACCESS POINTS AND PARKING:
The boardwalk access is the obvious entry and the only one with built-in shower setups at the entry. Hamlet Avenue (already cited for Thursday fireworks) is the local move beyond just fireworks: quieter sand, the same Atlantic, and free residential-street parking is much more findable than the boardwalk lots. Carolina Beach uses a paid parking app (Parkmobile-style, signs posted at the lots) for metered lots in season; free street parking exists if you walk a couple blocks, but it fills by mid-morning in season. Kure Beach has generally easier free parking. The Freeman Park 4x4 entry charges a separate vehicle pass for driving on the sand (don't park there unless you're going on the beach with a 4WD; they will tow). If a guest is staying at the Marriott Oceanfront for the con, they don't need to think about beach parking; it's right there.

DOG BEACH RULES (locals notice violations):
- Leash law year-round: dogs must be on a leash on the public beach, no exceptions. Off-leash gets you a citation.
- Summer hours restriction: April 1 through September 30, NO dogs on the beach between 9 AM and 5 PM. Early morning, evening, and night walks only. October through March: dogs allowed any time (still on a leash).
- Pick up after your dog. Bag stations are common; bring your own to be safe.
- Freeman Park (the 4x4 north end): dogs may be off-leash if under voice control, but it's busy and hot and there are vehicles; use judgment.
- Kure Beach mirrors the same leash rules and summer hours restriction.
If a guest lets a dog off-leash in front of the lifeguard stand at noon in July, somebody's going to say something. Tell them up front.

FREEMAN PARK (the 4x4 north end):
North end of the island. You can drive a 4WD vehicle right onto the sand. Locals go for sunrise or stargazing, not midday; midday is chaos. Two non-negotiable rules:
- Air your tires down to 20 psi or you WILL get stuck in soft sand. Nobody is coming to dig you out.
- Leave 10 feet of clearance from the dunes for emergency vehicles. Don't be that person.

BEACH LIFE NOTES:
- Lifeguards on duty daily Memorial Day weekend through Labor Day weekend on the boardwalk beach. Outside that window, swim at your own judgment.
- The boardwalk beach is the loud, ride-and-arcade family scene. Walk a few blocks south to the Hamlet Avenue access for quiet sand and the exact same ocean. (Same trick as the Thursday fireworks.)
- Kure Beach is quieter by design: no boardwalk amusements, no bars on the sand. The pier is the centerpiece. Kure Beach Fishing Pier (built 1923) is the oldest fishing pier on the East Coast; free to stroll, modest daily fee to fish (cheaper weekly passes if you're staying the week, ask at the shop). NC saltwater fishing license is required to fish public waters, BUT the pier's blanket license covers anyone fishing from it, a real perk if you don't have a license. What's biting changes by season: spring through early summer (the NaClCON window) is good for spot, croaker, mullet, occasional Spanish mackerel and bluefish; flounder picks up later in summer. Bait and tackle rentals at the pier shop. Coffee on the bench at dawn while pelicans coast past at eye level is the actual move whether or not you fish.
- Freeman Park (the 4x4 north end, already covered above) is the drive-on beach. Air tires down to 20 psi. Not midday.
- For paddleboarding on calm water instead of ocean swell, Carolina Beach Lake is right in town with a bike path around it; the Cape Fear River side is the other option. The rental shop can tell you where they're putting in that day.
- Don't haul a beach chair in a rental car. Both shops deliver to your cottage or directly to the sand.

SOUTH END (Fort Fisher area, 5-10 minutes further south than Kure):
- NC Aquarium at Fort Fisher, solid mid-sized aquarium: shark tank, sea turtle rehab tank (excellent followup after the nesting-season talk), an albino alligator named Luna, plus a conservatory of native NC wildlife. The rainy-day fallback for a conference attendee with a half-day to kill. Tickets at the door or online; AAA discount.
- Fort Fisher State Recreation Area, adjacent to the historic site and the aquarium, at the southern tip of the island. Quieter beach than Carolina Beach proper, with rocky outcrops (rare for NC), good shelling, and jetty fishing. The Cove (just south of the rocks) is where locals go for tidepool peeking and calmer swimming. A drive-on portion exists with a permit. The water sometimes turns surprisingly turquoise depending on currents.
- Fort Fisher historic site, the earthworks and the visitor center are free; great quiet walk around the largest Civil War earthen fortification on the continent. Already covered in the history section.
- Fort Fisher to Southport Ferry, NCDOT-run vehicle ferry crossing the mouth of the Cape Fear River. 30-35 minute ride, takes cars, runs roughly hourly during peak season (less off-season; check the NCDOT schedule). Scenic: you'll see ospreys, dolphins, container ships heading into the port of Wilmington, the Oak Island Lighthouse on the way. Cheap (a few bucks per vehicle). Locals use it as both a scenic loop AND a real commute. The half-day loop: drive to Fort Fisher, ferry to Southport, lunch in Southport (Provision Company is the cliche but it's actually good), ferry back. Done.

PLEASURE ISLAND HISTORY (because someone always asks):

THE NAME & THE FOUNDING:
"Pleasure Island" is the colloquial name for the barrier island that holds Carolina Beach, Kure Beach, and Fort Fisher. Carolina Beach proper was founded in 1857 by Joseph Winner, who laid out the town streets. The resort era kicked off when a steamship plus a small railroad started bringing weekenders down from Wilmington.

SNOW'S CUT (1930, when the peninsula became an actual island):
Pleasure Island wasn't always an island. The southern tip of the Cape Fear peninsula was attached to the mainland until 1930, when the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers cut a channel through it as part of the Intracoastal Waterway project. That cut, Snow's Cut, named after Major William A. Snow, is what makes "island" a literal description today. So when a guest tilts their head and asks "wait, is this actually an island," the answer is: yes, by way of 1930s-era earthmoving. The Snow's Cut Bridge on US-421 is the main crossing on and off the island; there's a small park and trailhead on the mainland side worth a quick walk if anyone's curious.

HURRICANE HAZEL (October 1954):
The before-and-after moment for the island. Hazel made landfall at the NC/SC border and leveled almost every structure on the boardwalk. The town that exists today is built from the debris and determination of the people who refused to leave. When you're walking the boardwalk, you're walking on a rebuild.

THE BOARDWALK'S GOLDEN AGE:
1940s-50s, big bands, dance pavilions, real boardwalk romance. That vintage soul is why places like Britt's Donuts (still glazed-only, still cash-only) survive intact. Locals don't change things just to change them.

FREEMAN FAMILY / NORTH END LEGACY:
The north end of the island carries the legacy of the Freeman family, free people of color before the Civil War. During Jim Crow, when most coastal beaches were closed to Black families, this stretch (historically known as Seabreeze / Freeman Beach) was one of the few places along the southern NC coast where Black families could come to the ocean. The Freeman name carries through to the modern 4x4 park.

FORT FISHER, "GIBRALTAR OF THE SOUTH":
Five miles south, at the tip of the island. During the Civil War, Fort Fisher was the Confederacy's largest earthen fort and the protection for the Cape Fear blockade runners feeding Wilmington, the South's last major open port. The Second Battle of Fort Fisher (January 1865) was one of the largest naval bombardments in history to that point: roughly 600 Union ships shelling the fort before the infantry assault. It fell January 15, 1865; Wilmington followed about a month later. Today it's a state historic site and a quietly haunted place to walk the earthworks.

  If a NaClCON guest wants the hacker analogy: Fort Fisher was the Confederacy's firewall protecting the blockade runners, the data packets, moving in and out of Wilmington. It held for years until a coordinated naval-and-infantry attack took it down. Packet flow stopped a month later. (Don't force this angle; only use it if someone's already on a tech-history wavelength.)

The local museum and archives. If a guest is genuinely interested in the area's history beyond beach trivia, send them here.

NC HACKER HISTORY (NaClCON-relevant context):

THE CONDOR IN RALEIGH (February 1995):
Kevin Mitnick, "The Condor," the world's most-wanted hacker at the time, was tracked down to an apartment in Raleigh by the FBI in February 1995, with Tsutomu Shimomura's help triangulating his cellular activity. He was running with a laptop and cellular phone tech. The case became a landmark for social engineering as the actual edge of the craft: the biggest hole was usually a person being too nice on the phone, not a buffer overflow. Mitnick himself was a BBS native; the NaClCON crowd considers him family in spirit.

THE BBS UNDERGROUND:
Before the modern web, the BBS scene was where the digital underground actually lived, dial-up modems screeching, text-mode boards, philes, ASCII art, message bases, doors, sysops who were either gods or nightmares. NC had a healthy 80s/90s scene like most states. NaClCON exists partly to preserve that aesthetic, and this BBS (the one the guest is currently logged into) is part of that preservation, not just nostalgia hardware.

THE TOWN'S OWN CYBERSECURITY LESSON (December 2025):
Carolina Beach itself got hit by two related cyberattacks in December 2025, the first discovered December 23, 2025, the second on January 6, 2026, losing roughly $488,000 total (~$200k + ~$287k) in fraudulent wire transfers. Investigators tied it to an ongoing international investigation targeting small local governments. The town's cyber insurance covered only $25,000; the rest came out of the general fund. Mayor Lynn Barbee stated publicly that staff followed all town policies. It's a cautionary tale that lands extra hard at a hacker conference: BEC and impersonation attacks work on small towns, big banks, and everyone in between. Worth mentioning when a guest asks about local security stories; a polite reminder to double-check sender addresses never hurts.

NACLCON ITSELF, "SALT CON":
NaCl is the chemical symbol for sodium chloride, table salt. NaClCON is pronounced "Salt Con." It's the island's hacker-archaeology conference: BBS culture, phreaking, the evolution of the digital underground, and how we got here. The 2026 con runs May 31 - June 2. Anyone on this BBS right now is most likely an attendee, a speaker, or someone tied to the con.

THE FIREWALL CONNECTION (a callback, only if it lands):
Fort Fisher was the 1860s firewall protecting Wilmington's ports. Today the people doing that job use code instead of cannons. Carolina Beach has been on a defensive perimeter for 160+ years.
